<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Your credit score is a three-digit number that quietly controls some of the biggest financial decisions in your life. It determines whether you get approved for a mortgage, what interest rate you pay on a car loan, whether a landlord accepts your rental application, and sometimes even whether you get a job offer.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Despite its importance, most people don't understand how their score is calculated—and the internet is full of myths that cause people to make counterproductive moves. Here's what actually matters.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Five Factors That Determine Your Score</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>FICO scores (used by 90% of lenders) range from 300-850 and are calculated from five weighted factors:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">1. Payment History — 35%</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>This is the biggest factor by far. Have you paid your bills on time? Late payments, collections, bankruptcies, and charge-offs all damage this category. A single 30-day late payment can drop a good score by 60-110 points.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>What to do:</strong> Set up autopay for at least the minimum payment on every account. Never miss a payment. If you do miss one, call the creditor immediately—many will remove the late mark if you pay quickly and have a good history.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">2. Credit Utilization — 30%</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>This is how much of your available credit you're using. If you have a $10,000 credit limit and carry a $3,000 balance, your utilization is 30%.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Lower is better. Under 30% is acceptable. Under 10% is ideal. 0% isn't perfect—having a small balance that you pay in full each month is slightly better than never using your cards at all.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>What to do:</strong> Pay your credit card balance before the statement closing date (not just the due date) to report a lower utilization. Request credit limit increases on existing cards—this lowers your utilization ratio without changing your spending.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">3. Length of Credit History — 15%</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The average age of your accounts and the age of your oldest account both matter. This is why closing old credit cards can hurt your score—it shortens your average account age.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>What to do:</strong> Keep your oldest credit card open, even if you rarely use it. Put a small recurring charge on it (like a streaming subscription) and set up autopay so it stays active.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">4. Credit Mix — 10%</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Lenders like to see that you can handle different types of credit: revolving credit (credit cards), installment loans (car loans, student loans, mortgages), and retail accounts. Having a mix shows you can manage various types of debt responsibly.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>What to do:</strong> Don't open accounts just to improve your mix—that's counterproductive. But understand that having only credit cards or only installment loans gives you a slightly lower score than having both.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">5. New Credit Inquiries — 10%</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Each time you apply for credit, a "hard inquiry" appears on your report. One or two inquiries barely matter (5-10 point drop that recovers within months). But multiple applications in a short period signals desperation to lenders.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Exception:</strong> Rate shopping for mortgages, auto loans, or student loans within a 14-45 day window counts as a single inquiry. The scoring models expect you to shop around for these major loans.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>What to do:</strong> Only apply for credit you actually need. Space applications at least 6 months apart when possible.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Credit Score Ranges and What They Mean</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>800-850 (Exceptional):</strong> Best rates on everything. You're in the top tier</li>
<li><strong>740-799 (Very Good):</strong> Qualifies for most premium offers. Functionally similar to 800+ for most lenders</li>
<li><strong>670-739 (Good):</strong> Approved for most products but not always at the best rates</li>
<li><strong>580-669 (Fair):</strong> Subprime territory. Higher rates, fewer options, may need secured cards</li>
<li><strong>300-579 (Poor):</strong> Difficulty getting approved. Focus on rebuilding before applying for new credit</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The practical difference between 740 and 800 is minimal—most lenders offer the same rates above 740. Focus on getting above 740, not on reaching a perfect 850.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Common Credit Score Myths</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Myth: Checking your own credit hurts your score.</strong><br>False. Checking your own score is a "soft inquiry" and has zero impact. Check it as often as you want. Free options include Credit Karma, your bank's app, or annualcreditreport.com for full reports.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Myth: Carrying a balance helps your score.</strong><br>False. This is one of the most expensive myths in personal finance. You never need to pay interest to build credit. Use your card, pay it in full every month, and your score benefits just as much.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Myth: Closing credit cards improves your score.</strong><br>Usually false. Closing cards reduces your total available credit (increasing utilization) and eventually shortens your credit history. Both hurt your score.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Myth: Income affects your credit score.</strong><br>False. Your income doesn't appear on your credit report and isn't part of the score calculation. A person earning $30,000 with perfect payment history can have a higher score than someone earning $300,000 with missed payments.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Myth: You need to be in debt to have good credit.</strong><br>False. You need to use credit responsibly—not carry debt. A credit card paid in full every month builds excellent credit without costing you a penny in interest.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">How to Build Credit from Scratch</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If you have no credit history (common for young adults, recent immigrants, or anyone who's avoided credit), here's the playbook:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} -->
<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Get a secured credit card.</strong> You put down a deposit (usually $200-500) that becomes your credit limit. Use it for a small recurring purchase, pay in full monthly. After 6-12 months of responsible use, most issuers upgrade you to an unsecured card and return your deposit</li>
<li><strong>Become an authorized user.</strong> Ask a family member with good credit to add you to one of their cards. Their payment history on that card gets added to your credit report. You don't even need to use the card</li>
<li><strong>Use a credit-builder loan.</strong> Companies like Self offer small loans specifically designed to build credit history. The money goes into a savings account while you make payments, then you get it back at the end</li>
</ol>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">How to Improve an Existing Score</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Fastest impact actions, in order:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} -->
<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Pay down credit card balances</strong> to below 10% utilization (can improve score within 30 days)</li>
<li><strong>Dispute any errors</strong> on your credit reports at annualcreditreport.com (30-45 days to resolve)</li>
<li><strong>Set up autopay on everything</strong> to prevent future late payments</li>
<li><strong>Request credit limit increases</strong> on existing cards (instant utilization improvement)</li>
<li><strong>Keep old accounts open</strong> and active with small charges</li>
<li><strong>Stop applying for new credit</strong> temporarily (let inquiries age off)</li>
</ol>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Most people can improve their score by 50-100 points within 3-6 months by focusing on utilization and payment history alone.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">When Your Credit Score Matters Most</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The financial impact of your credit score is most significant for large loans:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Mortgage example:</strong> On a $300,000 30-year mortgage, the rate difference between a 760 score and a 660 score could be 0.5-1.0%. That's $30,000-60,000 more in interest over the life of the loan. Improving your score before buying a home is one of the highest-value financial moves you can make.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Auto loan example:</strong> A 720+ score might get you 4-5% APR. A 620 score might mean 10-14% APR. On a $25,000 car loan over 5 years, that difference is $4,000-7,000 in extra interest.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Your Action Plan</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} -->
<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li>Check your credit score for free (Credit Karma, your bank's app, or Discover's free scorecard)</li>
<li>Pull your full credit reports at annualcreditreport.com and review for errors</li>
<li>Set up autopay for minimum payments on all accounts</li>
<li>Calculate your credit utilization—if above 30%, make paying it down a priority</li>
<li>Identify one action from the improvement list above and implement it this week</li>
</ol>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Your credit score isn't a measure of your worth—it's a tool. Like any tool, it works better when you understand how to use it. A few strategic moves now can save you tens of thousands of dollars over your lifetime on mortgages, car loans, and insurance premiums. That's a return on effort that's hard to beat.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
