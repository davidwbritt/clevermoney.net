<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>You've decided to get serious about paying off debt. You've cut expenses, maybe picked up extra income, and you have money to throw at your balances. But which debt do you attack first? This single decision can mean the difference between staying motivated for years or giving up after months.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Two strategies dominate the debt payoff conversation: the debt snowball and the debt avalanche. Both work. But one might work significantly better for you depending on your personality and situation.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Debt Snowball Method</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Popularized by Dave Ramsey, the snowball method is simple: pay minimum payments on everything, then throw all extra money at your smallest balance first. When that's paid off, roll that payment into the next smallest balance. Repeat until debt-free.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Example</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Say you have these debts and $500 extra per month to put toward payoff:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Credit Card A: $800 balance, 22% APR, $25 minimum</li>
<li>Credit Card B: $3,200 balance, 18% APR, $65 minimum</li>
<li>Car Loan: $8,500 balance, 6% APR, $280 minimum</li>
<li>Student Loan: $15,000 balance, 5% APR, $175 minimum</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Snowball order:</strong> Credit Card A → Credit Card B → Car Loan → Student Loan</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>You'd pay $525 toward Credit Card A ($500 extra + $25 minimum) while making minimums on everything else. Credit Card A is gone in less than 2 months. That quick win feels incredible.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Why It Works Psychologically</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Quick wins build momentum.</strong> Paying off that first debt in weeks instead of months creates a dopamine hit that keeps you going</li>
<li><strong>Visible progress.</strong> Watching debts disappear from your list is deeply satisfying</li>
<li><strong>Simplification.</strong> Fewer accounts to manage, fewer minimum payments to track</li>
<li><strong>Behavioral research backs it up.</strong> A Harvard Business Review study found people who focused on small balances first were more likely to eliminate all their debt</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Debt Avalanche Method</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The avalanche method is the mathematician's approach: pay minimums on everything, then throw all extra money at the debt with the highest interest rate. When that's paid off, move to the next highest rate.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Same Example, Different Order</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Using the same debts:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Avalanche order:</strong> Credit Card A (22%) → Credit Card B (18%) → Car Loan (6%) → Student Loan (5%)</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In this case, the order happens to be similar because the smallest balance also has the highest rate. But imagine Credit Card A had a $5,000 balance and the car loan was $800—the avalanche would still target the 22% credit card first, while the snowball would knock out the car loan.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Why It Works Mathematically</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Minimizes total interest paid.</strong> By attacking the highest-rate debt first, you reduce the most expensive debt fastest</li>
<li><strong>Faster overall payoff.</strong> You'll typically be debt-free weeks to months sooner than with the snowball</li>
<li><strong>More money stays in your pocket.</strong> The interest savings can be hundreds or thousands of dollars depending on your balances</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Real Difference: Math vs. Motivation</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Here's the honest truth: the avalanche saves you more money. Always. That's just math. But the snowball has a higher completion rate. That's just psychology.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The difference in total interest between the two methods is often smaller than people expect—typically 5-15% of total interest paid. On $27,500 in debt like our example, that might be $300-$800 over the entire payoff period.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Meanwhile, the cost of quitting your debt payoff plan entirely? Thousands of dollars in ongoing interest, potentially for years.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>The best debt payoff strategy is the one you'll actually stick with.</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Which Method Is Right for You?</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Choose the snowball if:</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>You've tried paying off debt before and quit</li>
<li>You need visible progress to stay motivated</li>
<li>You have several small debts you can eliminate quickly</li>
<li>Your interest rates are relatively similar across debts</li>
<li>You're feeling overwhelmed by the number of debts</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Choose the avalanche if:</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>You're disciplined and data-driven</li>
<li>You have one debt with a significantly higher interest rate than the others</li>
<li>You don't need quick wins to stay committed</li>
<li>Saving money on interest is more motivating than crossing debts off a list</li>
<li>Your highest-rate debt also happens to be one of your smaller balances</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Hybrid Approach</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Nobody says you have to pick one method exclusively. A practical hybrid approach:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} -->
<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li>If any debt is under $500, snowball it first for a quick win regardless of interest rate</li>
<li>After clearing small balances, switch to avalanche for remaining debts</li>
<li>If you ever feel motivation slipping, temporarily switch to snowball to get a win</li>
</ol>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The rigid "you must do it this way" advice ignores that debt payoff is a marathon, not a sprint. Adapt your strategy to what keeps you running.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Moves That Matter More Than Method</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Whichever strategy you choose, these actions have a bigger impact than the snowball-vs-avalanche decision:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>1. Stop adding new debt.</strong> Cut up credit cards or freeze them (literally—in a block of ice). No payoff strategy works if you're adding to your balances.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>2. Call and negotiate rates.</strong> A 5-minute call to your credit card company asking for a lower rate succeeds about 70% of the time. Even a 2% reduction saves real money.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>3. Consider a balance transfer.</strong> A 0% APR balance transfer card can eliminate interest entirely for 12-21 months. This makes either payoff method dramatically more effective.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>4. Increase the gap.</strong> Finding an extra $200/month to throw at debt—through side income, cutting expenses, or both—matters more than which debt you target first.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>5. Track your progress visually.</strong> Print a debt payoff chart, use an app, or create a spreadsheet. Seeing the numbers drop keeps you engaged regardless of method.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Your Action Plan</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} -->
<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li>List every debt: balance, interest rate, and minimum payment</li>
<li>Calculate how much extra you can put toward debt each month</li>
<li>Choose your method: snowball, avalanche, or hybrid</li>
<li>Set up automatic payments so you can't forget or talk yourself out of it</li>
<li>Check your progress monthly and celebrate milestones</li>
</ol>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The fact that you're reading this means you're ready to take debt seriously. Whether you choose the snowball or avalanche, you're already ahead of everyone who's still making minimum payments and hoping for the best. Pick a method, commit to it, and start this week.</p>
