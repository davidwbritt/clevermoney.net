<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The biggest myth in investing is that you need a lot of money to start. You don't. With $100 and a smartphone, you can open a brokerage account and buy your first investment today. The real cost of waiting isn't the money you don't invest—it's the compound growth you miss.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why Starting Small Beats Waiting Until You Have More</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Consider two people. Person A invests $100/month starting at age 25. Person B waits until 35, then invests $200/month. By age 65, assuming 8% average annual returns:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Person A:</strong> Invested $48,000 total → Worth approximately $349,000</li>
<li><strong>Person B:</strong> Invested $72,000 total → Worth approximately $298,000</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Person A invested less money but ended up with more wealth. That's compound interest—your earnings generate their own earnings, and time is the most powerful variable in the equation. Every year you wait costs you more than any amount you could add later.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Before You Invest: The Prerequisites</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Investing with $100 is smart. Investing your last $100 is not. Make sure you have these basics covered first:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} -->
<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>A starter emergency fund</strong> ($500-$1,000 minimum)</li>
<li><strong>No high-interest debt</strong> actively growing (credit cards at 20%+ APR should be addressed first)</li>
<li><strong>Stable income</strong> covering your essential expenses</li>
</ol>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If you have high-interest debt, paying it off is effectively a guaranteed return equal to the interest rate. Paying off a 22% credit card is like earning 22% on your money, risk-free. No investment reliably beats that.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Step 1: Choose Your Account Type</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Where you invest matters almost as much as what you invest in, because of taxes.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">If Your Employer Offers a 401(k) Match — Start Here</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A 401(k) employer match is free money. If your company matches 50% up to 6% of your salary, that's an instant 50% return before your investments even grow. Always contribute enough to get the full match. Always.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Roth IRA — Best for Most Beginners</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A Roth IRA lets you invest after-tax dollars, but all growth and withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. If you're in a lower tax bracket now than you expect to be later, the Roth is usually the better choice. You can contribute up to $7,000/year (2025 limit) and withdraw your contributions (not gains) penalty-free anytime.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Taxable Brokerage Account — Maximum Flexibility</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>No tax advantages, but no restrictions either. No contribution limits, no withdrawal penalties, no income limits. Good for money you might need before retirement or after maxing out tax-advantaged accounts.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Recommended order:</strong> 401(k) up to match → Roth IRA → Taxable brokerage</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Step 2: Pick a Brokerage</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>You need a brokerage account to invest. The major brokerages have eliminated trading commissions and minimum balance requirements, making them all viable for small investors. Good options include:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Fidelity:</strong> No minimums, excellent research tools, fractional shares</li>
<li><strong>Charles Schwab:</strong> No minimums, great customer service, wide selection</li>
<li><strong>Vanguard:</strong> Pioneer of low-cost index investing, slightly dated interface but excellent funds</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>All three are established, reputable firms with SIPC insurance protecting your accounts. Don't overthink this decision—pick one and open an account. You can always transfer later.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Step 3: Understand What You're Buying</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>You don't need to pick individual stocks. In fact, you probably shouldn't. Here are the building blocks:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Index Funds and ETFs</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>An index fund holds every stock in a market index (like the S&P 500) in a single investment. When you buy an S&P 500 index fund, you own a piece of the 500 largest U.S. companies—Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, and 497 others.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Why index funds are ideal for beginners:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Instant diversification.</strong> One purchase spreads your risk across hundreds of companies</li>
<li><strong>Low costs.</strong> Expense ratios of 0.03-0.10% vs. 1-2% for actively managed funds</li>
<li><strong>Proven performance.</strong> Over any 20-year period in history, the S&P 500 has always produced positive returns</li>
<li><strong>No research required.</strong> You don't need to analyze earnings reports or predict winners</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Target-Date Funds</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If you want the simplest possible approach, a target-date fund automatically adjusts your stock/bond mix as you age. Pick the fund closest to your retirement year (e.g., Target 2060 if you're in your mid-20s) and invest everything there. It's a genuinely solid one-fund solution.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Step 4: Make Your First Investment</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>With $100, here are your best options:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Option A: One total stock market index fund</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>VTI (Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF) or FSKAX (Fidelity Total Market Index)</li>
<li>Covers the entire U.S. stock market—large, mid, and small companies</li>
<li>Put all $100 here</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Option B: One S&P 500 index fund</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>VOO (Vanguard S&P 500 ETF) or FXAIX (Fidelity 500 Index)</li>
<li>Covers the 500 largest U.S. companies</li>
<li>Slightly less diversified than total market but very similar performance</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Option C: A target-date fund</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Automatically includes U.S. stocks, international stocks, and bonds</li>
<li>Adjusts over time—more stocks when you're young, more bonds as you approach retirement</li>
<li>Truly set-it-and-forget-it</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Any of these options is an excellent choice. The differences between them are minor compared to the difference between investing and not investing.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Step 5: Automate and Keep Going</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Your first $100 is a start, not a destination. Set up automatic investments—even $25 or $50 per paycheck. This practice, called dollar-cost averaging, means you buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when prices are high, smoothing out market volatility over time.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The target: increase your contributions by 1% of your income every six months until you're investing 15-20% of your income. You won't miss money you never see in your checking account.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What NOT to Do</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Don't try to time the market.</strong> Nobody consistently predicts highs and lows. Missing just the 10 best days in the stock market over 20 years cuts your returns in half</li>
<li><strong>Don't check your portfolio daily.</strong> Markets go up and down constantly. Check quarterly at most</li>
<li><strong>Don't panic sell during downturns.</strong> Every market crash in history has been followed by a recovery. Selling during a dip locks in losses</li>
<li><strong>Don't pick individual stocks with your first $100.</strong> Diversification through index funds is how you manage risk when starting out</li>
<li><strong>Don't pay for financial advice at this stage.</strong> A simple index fund strategy doesn't require a financial advisor charging 1% of your assets annually</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Your Action Plan</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} -->
<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li>Check if your employer offers a 401(k) match—if so, sign up and contribute enough to get the full match</li>
<li>Open a Roth IRA at Fidelity, Schwab, or Vanguard (10 minutes online)</li>
<li>Deposit $100 and buy a total stock market index fund or target-date fund</li>
<li>Set up automatic monthly contributions from your checking account</li>
<li>Increase contributions by 1% every six months</li>
</ol>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The hardest part of investing is starting. Once your first $100 is working for you, you've crossed the psychological barrier that stops most people. From here, it's just repetition and patience—two things that cost nothing but build everything.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
